Start with understanding

A diagnosis is information. It is not the whole story.

Genetic conditions can affect health, development, or both. Each condition is different, and children with the same diagnosis can have different needs and strengths.

01

What the words mean

Genes carry instructions used by the body. A genetic condition can happen when a gene or chromosome works differently. A health professional can explain what a particular result means for your child.

02

How it is identified

Families may learn about a condition through screening, a medical evaluation, or genetic testing. Testing can offer useful information, but it does not always give a clear answer or predict exactly how a child will develop.

03

What comes next

A care plan may involve a primary care clinician and other specialists. Ask who coordinates care, which concerns need follow-up, and how to reach the team between visits.

04

Room for the child

Alongside medical needs, make space for play, friendships, learning, rest, and the things your child enjoys. Support should help a child participate in life, not reduce them to a list of appointments.

Support for everyday life

Small, steady steps can make a difference.

Families do not have to solve everything at once. Start with what is most useful this week, then build from there with your child's care team.

01

Make care easier to follow

Keep a simple list of medicines, appointments, questions, and the names of the people involved in care. Ask the team for a written summary when it helps.

02

Ask about daily supports

Talk with your child's clinician about therapies, developmental services, school accommodations, or community programs that may fit your child's needs.

03

Include your child

Use words and explanations that fit your child's age and communication style. Invite their preferences and questions whenever possible.

04

Look after caregivers

It is okay to ask someone you trust for a practical hand. Ask your care team about respite, social work, or caregiver support options in your area.

05

Make space for siblings

Siblings may have their own questions and feelings. Offer honest, age-appropriate conversations and time together that is not about medical care.

06

Connect at your pace

Another family may understand parts of the experience, but every family's situation is personal. Choose peer support that feels safe and right for you.

Not sure where to start? Write down one concern and bring it to your child's next appointment. You can ask the clinic who is best placed to help.

A little preparation

Make room for your questions.

Appointments can move quickly. Pick the prompts that matter to you and bring them along. It is okay to ask for plain language or to pause and take notes.

Common questions

A few helpful starting points.

Are all genetic conditions inherited?

No. Some genetic conditions are inherited from a parent, while others can happen for the first time in a child. A genetic counselor or clinician can explain what a specific result may mean for your family.

Does a genetic test explain everything?

Not always. A test may identify a cause, offer clues, or come back without a clear answer. The meaning depends on the test and your child's health history, so review results with a qualified health professional.

Where can I find help for my child's needs?

Start with your child's primary care clinician or specialist. Depending on your child's situation, they may connect you with a genetic counselor, therapist, social worker, school support, or local family organization.

How should I talk with my child about a diagnosis?

Use honest, simple language that fits your child's age and understanding. You can share information a little at a time, invite questions, and ask your care team for help finding words that suit your child.

A note about care

This website offers general educational information only. It cannot diagnose, treat, or replace advice from your child's healthcare professional. Medical needs vary; discuss questions about symptoms, testing, treatment, or care with a qualified clinician. If your child may be in immediate danger or needs urgent medical attention, contact your local emergency service.
